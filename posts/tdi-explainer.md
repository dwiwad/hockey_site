Trying to understand why one team wins while another falters a perennial focus of hockey punditry and sports stats nerds alike. When we look for explanations, we often move past simple statistics and into complex machine learning or other predictive models. Pundits also like to talk about the intangibles. Things we can speculate on, subjectively observe, but seldom measure with a strong degree of certainty. We talk about things like a team's "chemistry," the "momentum" they are displaying, or the "depth" of their roster.

When it comes to depth, commentators refer to aspects of a team's performance like "rolling four lines" or "bottom six ice time." But for as much as we talk about depth, I have not come across an methodical attempt to define or measure it. Is it just having a high-scoring fourth line? Is it how evenly ice time is distributed? Is it anything that's not two all-star players hogging the share of time and points?

Luckily for me, I spent the better part of my academic career thinking about how we measure these kind of unobservable constructs. Psychologists, by definition, can't directly observe what they need to measure (you can't simply see "depression" in the same way you see, for example, the time the sun rises in the morning). So, to me, this presents an interesting opportunity. Specifically, can we lean on common methods from statistics and psychology to define, measure, and utilize unobservable constructs like depth? 

## Conceptualizing Depth in the NHL

Depth in the NHL is often talked about in reference to the contributions of the bottom six

conceptualized as the extent to which a team’s performance is distributed across its players rather than concentrated within a few individuals. A team with high depth can maintain competitive performance even when star players are unavailable, lines are reshuffled, or game contexts shift, because contributions to success are not driven entirely by the top lines or defensive pairings. In contrast, a team with low depth relies disproportionately on a small subset of players whose absence or underperformance can sharply diminish overall team performance. In the context of the NHL, this distributional quality manifests across observable dimensions such as ice time, shot generation, and point production. Each of these represents a measurable indicator of the latent construct of depth; that is, how evenly a team’s capabilities are spread throughout its roster.
